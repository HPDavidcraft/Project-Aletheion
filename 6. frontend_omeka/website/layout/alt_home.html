FICTIONAL CASE FILE · SALTGLASS REACH

Reading a fictional excavation

Imagine a fictional inlet where a dark stain crosses a pale floor, a scatter of broken pottery rests beside it, and a thin charcoal lens sits underneath. The scene is invented, but the questions are real archaeological questions: did the stain form before or after the floor, did the sherds arrive with the same activity, and what can a sample actually date?

A single object cannot answer those questions by itself. Excavators record the location, boundaries, and relationships of each context before interpreting it. A hearth, storage pit, or workshop is a hypothesis supported by evidence—not a label to attach to an ambiguous patch of soil.

This page uses a made-up site to demonstrate how Pleroma could keep observations, interpretations, dates, and media linked but distinct. Every named place and measurement in the case file is synthetic.

1 · Read the layers

Stratigraphic relationships can establish relative order when deposits are intact. A cut, burrow, erosion surface, or later disturbance can interrupt that sequence, so the relationship itself must be recorded.

2 · Separate sample from event

A laboratory result dates the sampled material under stated assumptions; it does not automatically date the building, artifact, or activity beside it. Calibration, context, and possible residual or intrusive material matter.

3 · Keep alternatives visible

The same deposit might reflect a short visit, repeated seasonal use, or a longer occupation. Pleroma’s guideline calls for uncertainty and interpretation to remain explicit rather than presenting a hypothesis as fact.

CORE PRINCIPLES

HOW IT WORKS

01

Stratigraphy describes relationships

Archaeologists distinguish deposits, cuts, surfaces, and fills because their physical relationships can reveal relative sequence. “Lower” is not automatically “older” if a pit cuts through earlier layers or material has moved. The fictional inlet records those observations first, then stores any explanation as a separate hypothesis.

02

A date belongs to a sample

Radiocarbon analysis estimates when once-living material stopped exchanging carbon with its environment. Calibration converts a measured result into calendar-age probabilities; the dated seed or charcoal may pre-date its deposit or enter it later. Context and sample history are part of the argument, not footnotes.

03

Objects need context

Shape, material, wear, and manufacturing traces help describe an object. Similar forms can arise through exchange, imitation, shared constraints, or coincidence; typological resemblance alone does not establish a people, language, or trade route.

04

Environmental evidence has baselines

Pollen, animal remains, sediments, and isotopes can help reconstruct environments, diet, or mobility. Their interpretation depends on preservation, sampling strategy, local baselines, and comparison material; a single proxy rarely answers a broad social question by itself.

05

Images are evidence too

A photograph or plan needs a parent record, caption, coverage statement, rights information, and a source. Images on this page are local placeholders; none has been connected to a verified site or object record.

06

Interpretations can change

A good research record preserves the evidence supporting a claim, reasonable alternatives, and the history of revisions. New samples, re-analysis, or a better contextual link can change an interpretation without erasing the earlier reasoning.

“One dark stain becomes a hearth only after its edges, contents, context, and alternatives are examined.”

FICTIONAL FIELDWORK SEQUENCE · ALL EVENTS INVENTED

How a case file could develop

SIM-STEP 01

Describe before naming

At the imaginary Saltglass Reach, a dark circular stain is photographed, measured, and recorded as a feature. “Hearth” is held back as a hypothesis until its contents and boundaries are examined.

SIM-STEP 02

Link the contexts

A fictional floor appears to run beneath the stain, while a narrow cut crosses the floor. The record stores these observed relationships separately from the proposed sequence.

SIM-STEP 03

Choose a sample carefully

A simulated charcoal sample is assigned a sample ID, context link, material description, and collection note. The page has no laboratory result; the example exists to demonstrate the data pathway.

SIM-STEP 04

Represent a date as a range

The table’s “demo intervals” are fabricated labels, not EDTF dates or calibrated results. In a real record, the original measurement, calibration, uncertainty, and source would remain traceable.

SIM-STEP 05

Compare explanations

One fictional model proposes short visits; another proposes repeated seasonal use. Both should point to supporting and opposing evidence, and neither should be rendered as an established fact.

SIM-STEP 06

Preserve the revision

If later analysis changes the preferred explanation, a versioned audit trail records why. The earlier hypothesis remains visible as part of the research history rather than silently disappearing.
